<Person1> "Welcome back. Episode nine: governance, part two. Management groups, subscriptions, tags, quotas, cost management, and the Advisor blade. Questions covered: sixty two, sixty three, sixty four and ninety. Since governance is your weakest area, I'll also give you a map of which portal blade answers which kind of question, because question ninety was exactly a blade selection mistake."
</Person1><Person2> "Start with the hierarchy."
</Person2><Person1> "Azure organizes things in four levels. Management groups at the top, then subscriptions, then resource groups, then resources. Every tenant has a root management group, created automatically, whose ID is the tenant ID. You can create management groups beneath it, nested up to six levels deep, not counting the root and not counting the subscription level. A management group can contain subscriptions and other management groups. A subscription belongs to exactly one management group at a time. A management group can have only one parent. Policies and RBAC assignments at a management group flow down to everything beneath it. That's their purpose: apply a rule once to many subscriptions, for example, allow only West Europe and North Europe for the entire production management group."
</Person1><Person2> "What's a subscription for, then?"
</Person2><Person1> "A subscription is a billing boundary and a limits boundary, and an access boundary. Billing: costs are reported and invoiced per subscription. Limits: quotas, such as vCPUs per region, are per subscription. Access: RBAC assignments at the subscription scope apply to everything inside. Organizations create separate subscriptions for production and development, for different departments, or to avoid hitting limits. A subscription trusts one Entra tenant, and can be moved to a different tenant, a process called transferring directory, which breaks role assignments."
</Person1><Person2> "Resource groups?"
</Person2><Person1> "Logical containers for related resources that share a lifecycle: you deploy, manage, and delete them together. Every resource lives in exactly one resource group. The resource group has a location, which stores the metadata, but the resources inside can be in other regions. Deleting a resource group deletes everything inside, unless locks block it. Resource groups can't be nested."
</Person1><Person2> "Tags now."
</Person2><Person1> "Tags are name and value pairs attached to resources, resource groups and subscriptions to organize and report. For example, Environment equals Production, CostCenter equals Finance, Owner equals Anna. Limits: up to fifty tag name and value pairs per resource, resource group or subscription. Tag names up to five hundred twelve characters, values up to two hundred fifty six. Tag names are not case sensitive, values are. Key rule: tags are not inherited. If you tag a resource group, its resources do not automatically get the tag. To get inheritance, you use Azure Policy, with the built in definitions Inherit a tag from the resource group, or Require a tag and its value. Tags are used in Cost Management for grouping spend, in automation to target resources, and in Policy conditions. Tags do not affect permissions, they are metadata. Note: some resource types don't support tags, and you can't tag by default on classic resources."
</Person1><Person2> "Quotas, questions sixty two, sixty three, sixty four. Explain the setup."
</Person2><Person1> "Subscription one has vCPU quotas in West US. Standard B S family vCPUs: twenty. Standard D family vCPUs: twenty. Total regional vCPUs: twenty. Existing VMs: VM one, size Standard B two ms, two vCPUs, running. VM two, Standard B sixteen ms, sixteen vCPUs, stopped and deallocated. You plan to deploy new VMs, and the exam asks, one at a time, whether each can be deployed to West US. VM three needs two vCPUs from the same B family. Question sixty two: can you deploy VM three? True. VM four needs four vCPUs. Question sixty three: False. VM five needs sixteen vCPUs. Question sixty four: False. You got all three right."
</Person1><Person2> "Walk me through the arithmetic and the rule."
</Person2><Person1> "Quotas are enforced at two levels, and both must pass. Level one, the VM family quota, here the B family at twenty. Level two, the total regional vCPU quota, twenty across all families. Current usage per the exam: VM one, two, plus VM two, sixteen, equals eighteen. The exam counts the deallocated VM against the quota, so eighteen of twenty used. VM three adds two: twenty, which equals the limit, not exceeding it. Allowed, as the quota is a maximum. VM four adds four: twenty two, exceeds the regional cap, denied. VM five adds sixteen: thirty four, denied. The key idea: remaining capacity is the smaller of the two remaining numbers, family remaining and regional remaining. Always compute both. A deployment can fail because of the family quota even when the regional quota has space, or vice versa."
</Person1><Person2> "Is it really true that a deallocated VM counts?"
</Person2><Person1> "This is where I'd flag a real world difference. In real Azure, when a VM is stopped and deallocated, its compute resources are released, and I believe it stops counting against the vCPU usage that's measured for quota, though the disks and other resources remain. The exam's explanation says the opposite, that deallocated VMs still count. Because question sixty two to sixty four rely on that assumption, take the exam's logic for the exam: count all provisioned VMs in the region, running or deallocated. For real life, check the Usage plus quotas blade in the subscription, which shows current usage by family, and verify with a test. Either way, the method is identical: sum existing, add new, compare with each limit."
</Person1><Person2> "How do I increase a quota?"
</Person2><Person1> "In the portal, go to Subscriptions, then your subscription, then Usage plus quotas, choose the quota, and request an increase. Many quota increases are approved automatically, some need a support request. Quotas are per region and per subscription. Another way to get headroom: deploy in a different region, deploy to another subscription, or choose a size from a family that still has capacity."
</Person1><Person2> "Cost management and question ninety."
</Person2><Person1> "Question ninety: you have a subscription with one hundred virtual machines. You need to quickly identify VMs that are underutilized and could be moved to a lower cost tier. Which portal blade? Answer: Advisor. You chose Metrics. The options: Monitor, Advisor, Metrics, Customer insights."
</Person1><Person2> "Why Advisor?"
</Person2><Person1> "Azure Advisor is a free, built in recommendation engine. It analyzes your usage and configuration telemetry, then gives personalized recommendations across five pillars: Reliability, Security, Operational excellence, Performance, and Cost. For cost, it flags VMs with low utilization, for example average CPU under five percent for fourteen days, and recommends shutting down or resizing to a smaller SKU. It also suggests buying reservations or savings plans, deleting idle resources, and so on. With one hundred VMs, you wouldn't open each one's metrics; you want a list. Advisor is the list."
</Person1><Person2> "And why not Monitor or Metrics?"
</Person2><Person1> "Monitor is the umbrella for collecting and analyzing telemetry, and Metrics is the chart explorer within it, where you plot raw numbers like percentage CPU for a selected resource. They give data, not recommendations, and you would have to examine each VM. Customer insights is a distractor with the vocabulary of analytics, related to Dynamics. So the question wording, quickly identify underutilized and lower cost, is the Advisor signature. Memory trigger: recommendations equals Advisor."
</Person1><Person2> "A map of blades, please."
</Person2><Person1> "Very useful. Advisor: recommendations for cost, security, reliability, performance, operational excellence. Monitor: central place for metrics, logs, alerts, insights. Metrics: chart raw numbers for a resource. Activity log: who did what to which resource, the control plane audit trail for ninety days. Service Health: Azure platform incidents, planned maintenance and health advisories that affect your subscriptions. Resource Health: health of one specific resource, available or unavailable. Cost Management plus Billing: spending analysis, budgets, cost alerts, exports. Policy: compliance state and assignments. Defender for Cloud: security posture and threat protection. Network Watcher: network diagnostics, connection monitor, NSG flow logs, IP flow verify, next hop. Log Analytics: query logs with KQL. If you can place the verb, you can place the blade."
</Person1><Person2> "Cost management in more depth?"
</Person2><Person1> "Cost analysis lets you view spend by resource group, tag, service, location. Budgets let you set a monthly, quarterly or annual amount and add alerts at thresholds, such as eighty percent actual or one hundred percent forecast, sending emails or triggering action groups. A budget doesn't stop spending by itself, it only alerts, unless you attach automation to act. Exports send cost data to a storage account on a schedule. Reservations give discounts for one or three year commitments on VMs, storage and more. Savings plans commit to an hourly spend. Azure Hybrid Benefit lets you use existing Windows Server and SQL Server licenses. Spot VMs give deep discounts for interruptible workloads. A dev and test setup might use auto shutdown on VMs, which is a simple cost control."
</Person1><Person2> "Twists on this episode?"
</Person2><Person1> "Ask which feature gives a notification when spending reaches eighty percent of the monthly amount: a budget with an alert. Ask where to see spend by department: cost analysis grouped by tag, if the resources are tagged. Ask how to apply a tag to all resources automatically: Policy with Modify or inherit tag. Ask how to apply a policy to ten subscriptions at once: management group. Ask what happens to policy assigned at a management group when you move a subscription out: it stops applying. Ask what's the maximum depth: six levels."
