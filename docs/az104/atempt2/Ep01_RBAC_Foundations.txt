Every question in that exam tests a specific idea, and in this series we unpack the idea behind every single one. Episode one is the foundation of the whole exam: how Azure decides who can do what, which is Azure role based access control, or RBAC."
</Person1><Person2> "And I keep hearing RBAC is the single biggest topic. Is that actually true for your exam set?"
</Person2><Person1> "Count them. Questions one, two, three, four, eight, nine, ten, nineteen to twenty two, thirty three to thirty five, forty five, forty six, fifty nine, sixty, sixty four to sixty eight, seventy three, seventy four, seventy six, seventy eight to eighty, eighty nine and ninety. </Person1><Person2> "Okay. Start from zero. What problem does RBAC solve?"
</Person2><Person1> "It answers one sentence: who can do what, and where. Every role assignment in Azure has exactly three parts. A security principal, which is the who. A role definition, which is the what. And a scope, which is the where. If you can say, this principal gets this role at this scope, you have described a role assignment completely."
</Person1><Person2> "Let's take the who first."
</Person2><Person1> "There are four kinds of security principal. A user. A group. A service principal, which is the identity of an application. And a managed identity, which is a special service principal that Azure manages for one of your resources. The best practice is to assign roles to groups, not individual users, because then you manage access by changing group membership. Question seventy eight shows this. Group1 had Reader at a management group, and every member of Group1 inherited the ability to view the Azure Functions."
</Person1><Person2> "And the what is the role definition."
</Person2><Person1> "Right. A role definition is a collection of permissions, and the anatomy of it appears directly in questions sixty seven and sixty eight. It has a name, a description, a role type which says built in or custom, and a permissions block. Inside permissions there are four lists. Actions, notActions, dataActions and notDataActions. Plus a separate property called assignableScopes. Let's go through each carefully, because the exam asks you to pick the right section."
</Person1><Person2> "Go."
</Person2><Person1> "Actions are control plane operations. The control plane is the management layer, Azure Resource Manager. Creating a virtual machine, deleting a storage account, changing a network rule, these all go through the control plane. An action is written as provider, then resource type, then operation, for example Microsoft dot Compute slash virtualMachines slash write. NotActions are subtracted from the actions list. DataActions are data plane operations, meaning what you do inside the resource, like reading the contents of a blob, or signing in to a virtual machine. NotDataActions subtract from dataActions. And assignableScopes says where this role is allowed to be assigned."
</Person1><Person2> "You said notActions are subtracted. Is that the same as a deny?"
</Person2><Person1> "This is one of the most common misunderstandings, so listen carefully. NotActions is not a deny. It is simply subtraction inside that one role. If a role has actions star and notActions Microsoft dot Authorization slash star slash write, then that role covers everything except authorization writes. But if the same person has a second role assignment that does grant authorization write, they get it. RBAC takes the union of everything granted. Real denies only come from deny assignments, which are created by Azure itself, for example by deployment stacks and managed applications, and you cannot author them directly as a normal user."
</Person1><Person2> "So RBAC is additive."
</Person2><Person1> "Completely additive. Question sixty four is built on this. Priya had Contributor on one network security group, Reader on the subscription, and Storage Account Contributor on a resource group. Can she create a storage account in that resource group? True, because Storage Account Contributor at resource group scope grants it. The narrower Contributor on the NSG doesn't override or reduce it. Everything adds together. There is no such thing as a lower scope role taking away a higher scope role."
</Person1><Person2> "Now the where. Scopes."
</Person2><Person1> "Four levels, top to bottom. Management group, subscription, resource group, resource. And above your own management groups sits the tenant root group, which every other management group and subscription lives under. Permissions flow downhill only. A role at the management group applies to every subscription beneath it, every resource group beneath those, and every resource beneath those. A role at a single resource applies to that resource and nothing else. Nothing flows upward and nothing flows sideways."
</Person1><Person2> "Give me the picture you gave me earlier."
</Person2><Person1> "A building. The management group is the building, a subscription is a floor, a resource group is a room, and a resource is a cabinet in the room. A master key to the building opens everything. A key to one cabinet opens only that cabinet. When the exam asks, can this person do X on Y, you locate Y in the building, then ask whether any of the person's keys sits on Y, or on something that contains Y."
</Person1><Person2> "What are the exact scope strings, in case I'm shown a role definition?"
</Person2><Person1> "A subscription scope looks like slash subscriptions slash the subscription id. A resource group scope adds slash resourceGroups slash the group name. A resource adds slash providers slash the provider slash the type slash the name. A management group is slash providers slash Microsoft dot Management slash managementGroups slash its id. In question sixty eight, the answer was assignableScopes, and you would put the resource group scope string there to restrict where the role can be assigned."
</Person1><Person2> "Let's cover the four classic roles, since everything compares to them."
</Person2><Person1> "Reader sees everything but changes nothing. Its action is star slash read. Contributor creates and manages all resources but cannot grant access. Technically it has actions star, with notActions that remove authorization write and delete, so it cannot assign roles. Owner has actions star, so it can do everything including assign roles. And User Access Administrator manages access only, with no right to create or manage the resources themselves."
</Person1><Person2> "Contributor builds but can't hand out keys."
</Person2><Person1> "Perfect. And a newer role sits next to User Access Administrator, called Role Based Access Control Administrator. It manages role assignments, and its special trick is that you can add a condition that limits which roles it can assign, and to whom. Question seventy four tested exactly this, and I'll come back to it."
</Person1><Person2> "Which questions test the idea that Contributor can't assign roles?"
</Person2><Person1> "Question forty six. Dana is Owner, Priya is Security Admin, Sam is Network Contributor. Who can assign the Reader role on the virtual network? Dana only. Network Contributor can manage the virtual network but cannot manage access. Security Admin works with security policies and Defender settings, not with who has access. Only Owner, User Access Administrator and Role Based Access Control Administrator have the Microsoft dot Authorization slash roleAssignments slash write permission, and that is the one permission you need to assign roles."
</Person1><Person2> "And the same scenario had a question about creating subnets."
</Person2><Person1> "Question forty five. Who can create a subnet? Dana and Sam. Owner has everything. Network Contributor has Microsoft dot Network slash star, so subnets are included. Security Admin has no network write. The wrong option said none, because it requires Global Administrator in Entra, which brings us to the second big concept."
</Person1><Person2> "Two worlds of roles."
</Person2><Person1> "Yes. There are two separate authorization systems. Microsoft Entra roles control the directory: users, groups, applications, licenses, password resets. Examples are Global Administrator, User Administrator, Groups Administrator. Azure RBAC roles control Azure resources: virtual machines, storage, networks. Examples are Owner, Contributor, Reader. A Global Administrator has no automatic permissions on Azure subscriptions. They can elevate themselves, using a switch in the Entra properties page called Access management for Azure resources, which grants them User Access Administrator at the root scope. Then they can give themselves access. But that is a deliberate, audited action, not a default."
</Person1><Person2> "So if a question says Global Administrator can create a subnet because they're Global Administrator, that's a trap."
</Person2><Person1> "A false statement. Entra roles manage identities. Azure roles manage resources. Hold that sentence."
</Person1><Person2> "Now control plane versus data plane. Why does it matter so much?"
</Person2><Person1> "Because it is the key to questions eight, nine, ten, seventy three and sixty seven. Management operations and data operations are authorized separately. A Contributor on a storage account can create the account, change its firewall and delete it, but the data inside, the blobs, are data plane. To read blobs through Entra you need a data role, like Storage Blob Data Reader. The same split exists for virtual machines. Virtual Machine Contributor lets you start, stop, resize and delete the VM. It does not let you log in to the operating system. To sign in with Entra credentials, you need Virtual Machine User Login, which is a regular user session, or Virtual Machine Administrator Login, which is a local administrator session. Those two roles contain dataActions."
</Person1><Person2> "Wait. You said earlier Contributor can't read data. But I thought anyone who can list storage keys can read the data."
</Person2><Person1> "Excellent catch, and it's the real world nuance. Contributor and Owner have the listKeys action on storage accounts. A storage account key is a shared secret that gives full data access. So technically a Contributor can retrieve a key and then read blobs using that key, bypassing Entra data roles. That is exactly why the role named Reader and Data Access exists. It gives read access to the management plane, plus the ability to list keys, so the person can reach data through the keys. In question seventy three, the exam used that role as the least privilege answer for viewing data in any storage account."
</Person1><Person2> "How did the exam treat Contributor then?"
</Person2><Person1> "As a management role that is not the designed way to read data and is over privileged. When two answers could work, the exam chooses the role designed for the job with the narrowest rights. We'll see that in the drills episode."
</Person1><Person2> "Give me the method. How do I solve any RBAC question?"
</Person2><Person1> "A three check method. Check one, scope. Trace each role assignment to the resource in the question. Does the assigned scope contain the resource? Check two, content. Does the role actually include the action that the question asks about? Not what the role is called, what it includes. Check three, plane. Is the action control plane or data plane, and does the role match that plane? If all three pass, the answer is true. If any fails, it's false. Say it with me: scope, content, plane."
</Person1><Person2> "And least privilege adds two more words."
</Person2><Person1> "Narrowest role, narrowest scope. When a question says least privilege, among all roles that work choose the one with the fewest permissions, and among all scopes that work choose the lowest. Question sixty is the perfect example, we'll go through it."
</Person1><Person2> "What else should I know about limits and mechanics?"
</Person2><Person1> "A few facts. Role assignments are per scope, and a subscription supports a few thousand role assignments, so you assign to groups to stay under the limit. Role assignment changes can take a few minutes to take effect, because tokens are cached. You can assign roles at the tenant root scope using elevated access. Assignments can be time limited and just in time with Privileged Identity Management, which needs Entra ID P2. And assignments can carry conditions, which you will see in episode three."

There is also a Roles tab showing built in definitions. From the command line, az role assignment list, and az role definition list. And you can see inherited assignments marked with their scope."
